## THE EIGHT METHODS OF FINANCING A STARTUP

`SOURCE FACT` — a recurring frame appears between each: **"Multiple methods often
used in a deal lifecycle. Not mutually exclusive options."** Several are tagged
**"Collateral/Cash Flow Required."**

### 1. Bootstrapping
- Using **personal savings, sweat equity, and early revenues**
- **Maximum control and ownership retained**
- **Forces lean, efficient operations**
- *Risk:* limited growth potential without outside capital

### 2. Friends and Family
- Early capital from personal network
- **Usually informal agreements (equity or loan)**
- **"Trust is critical, but risks relationships if venture fails"**
- Typically small amounts

### 3. Crowdfunding — **"up to $5M before SEC Regulated"**
- Raise small amounts from large groups online (**Kickstarter, GoFundMe**)
- Options: **donation, reward, debt, or equity-based**
- **"Great for testing market demand before launch"**
- Requires marketing effort & visibility

### 4. Incubators & Accelerators
- Mentorship, workspace, resources
- **Often exchange support for a small equity stake**
- Strong networking
- **May include a demo day to investors**

### 5. Government SBIR/STTR Grants
- U.S. programs for **R&D-heavy startups**
- **Non-dilutive funding (no equity given up)**
- **Highly competitive & paperwork heavy**
- Best for tech, biotech, engineering startups

### 6. Bank Loans — **"*NOT*"**
`PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW` — the heading itself carries the verdict:
- Traditional debt financing; **require collateral & credit history**
- **"Interest must be repaid regardless of success"**
- **"Often inaccessible for asset-light startups"**

Consistent with `SRC-P-007`'s capitalized correction on Compaq —
*"NO BANK LOANS AT ALL — MOVIE IS WRONG."* **The course is emphatic that banks
do not fund startups.**

### 7. Angel Investors
- **Wealthy individuals investing personal funds**
- Provide **capital + mentorship + connections**
- **Typically invest earlier than venture capitalists**
- High risk tolerance, want an equity stake
- **"THIS MAY BE THE FIRST STEP WITH PREFERRED STOCK THAT A VC INVESTS IN"**

### 8. Venture Capital
See below.

**The ladder, as taught:** bootstrap → friends & family → crowdfunding →
accelerator → grants → (not banks) → angels → VC. **Angels are the hinge** — the
first point where preferred stock enters, which is where `SRC-P-005`'s term-sheet
machinery starts to apply.

## HOW A VC FUND WORKS

`SOURCE FACT` (slide 24) — "WHAT IS VENTURE CAPITAL FUNDING?"

**LIMITED PARTNERS:** "Pension Funds, High Net Worth Offices, Insurance
Companies, **Corporate Venture Arms**"

**VCs are GENERAL PARTNERS:** "The VC firm as General Partner invests the fund;
**ideally with more expertise than the LP could do themselves.**"

| Term | Value |
|---|---|
| Management fee | **2% of the fund per year, irrespective of return** |
| Carried interest | **20% of proceeds after fund returned** |
| Fund length | **10 years** |
| **GP commitment** | **1% of fund must be the VCs' own money** |

**PORTFOLIO INVESTMENTS in PRIVATE COMPANIES: Deals.**

**The 1% GP commitment is new** — it does not appear anywhere else in the deck
set. It is the "skin in the game" requirement LPs impose, and a good detail to
know cold.

> **"A VC FUND is only for TEN YEARS....."** (slide 28) — stated as a standalone
> slide, i.e. the constraint that drives everything else.

### Fund size — `SOURCE FACT` (slide 31)
> "SIZE of VENTURE CAPITAL FUNDS (**Median is ~$150M**)
> New ('Emerging') Funds are done
> **Bigger is NOT better…..if they do not have good returns**

## WHAT IT COSTS TO REACH AN IPO

`SOURCE FACT` — posed as a question, then answered:

> "WHAT IS THE AVERAGE AMOUNT OF VENTURE CAPITAL REQUIRED TO GET TO AN IPO?"
>
> **"It usually takes $75M+ and at least 3 rounds of financing to get a company
> public…"**
>
> **"It typically takes ~8 years to get a company public (just under 10 year fund
> window)"**

**This is a crucial pairing.** The fund lives 10 years; the company takes ~8 to
go public. There is almost no slack — which explains the exit pressure in
`SRC-P-007`'s VC value-add table ("short-term exit pressure, diluted control")
and why `SRC-P-013`'s VC Method assumes a ~4-year hold on a mid-life investment.

### NVIDIA — `SOURCE FACT`
> "**NVIDIA…THE MOST VALUABLE COMPANY IN THE U.S — VC's THAT PUT IN $42M to TAKE
> IT PUBLIC IN 1999….**"

$42M of venture capital produced what is now the most valuable US company. **The
power law in a single line.**

### VCs don't hold public stock — `SOURCE FACT`, with a personal number
> "**VC's don't usually hold stock once public….**
> **I invested @ $4.80, 6 months before the IPO. 500%+ IRR at end of lock up.**"

A first-hand return figure. Connects to `SRC-P-007`: "VCs usually leave boards at
investment exit… **they should be doing more deals in their fund or raising a new
one.**"

## Exits

`SOURCE FACT` — "Go For the IPO, but usually sold along the way":
- **"~90% of the time a company in the venture world will be sold before an IPO"**
- **"Most times because they could not scale to IPO levels"**
- "Sometimes….competitors want them before they get public"
- **"In general….statistics show the IPO is the best return for investors and
  founders. That is the goal."**

Confirms `SRC-P-001`'s ~90% figure and adds the *reason*: failure to scale, not
preference.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Eight financing methods, not mutually exclusive** — bootstrapping through
   VC, with **banks explicitly ruled out** for startups.
2. **Angels are where preferred stock begins** — the hinge into term-sheet territory.
3. **Fund economics: 2% / 20% / 10 years / 1% GP commitment.** Median fund ~$150M,
   and **bigger is not better without returns.**
4. **$75M+ and 3+ rounds and ~8 years to an IPO** — against a 10-year fund life.
   The math is tight, and that tightness explains VC behavior.
5. **NVIDIA took $42M.** The Occam-style 24-year, 8-round, ~$150M loss is the
   same industry. Both are true.
6. **~90% are sold before IPO, mostly because they couldn't scale.**
7. **Misunderstanding the term sheet is the #1 source of founder/investor
   misalignment, especially at exit.**
