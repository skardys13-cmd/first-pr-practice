## Presentation Overview

The opening lecture. It does three jobs: administers the course, establishes the
instructor's credibility through his own investing record, and then delivers a
genuinely substantive overview of private markets — sizing them against public
markets, separating VC from PE, and laying out returns, exits, and deal sizes.

The second half is not throat-clearing. Slides 24–64 contain some of the most
quotable, interview-ready material in the entire deck set.

### VC vs PE — the course's central distinction

**Slide 30** — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`, flagged as a "QuickNote":
> "Venture Capital is subset of Private Equity — but **NOT the same thing.**
> VC firms and PE firms are **TOTALLY different processes**
> ……don't use those phrases incorrectly"

**Private Equity** (slide 54) — `SOURCE FACT`:
> "money invested into a private company, or the privatization of a company
> through the investment of outside money."
>
> PE Process Basic Steps: **invest into a company → take all/majority stake →
> improve the company exit value → exit their investment at a large profit.**
>
> "In order to magnify returns, PE firms may make use of leverage (borrowed
> money) to conduct Leveraged Buyouts (LBOs)."
>
> **"THE DOMAIN OF PROFITABLE COMPANIES PRIMARILY"**

**Venture Capital** (slide 55) — `SOURCE FACT`:
> "the financing startups and small business with exceptional growth potential
> receive in exchange for an equity stake."
>
> **"THE DOMAIN OF UNPROFITABLE, EARLY STAGE COMPANIES THAT NEED TO BE FINANCED
> THROUGH THE 'VALLEY OF DEATH'"**

**Slide 56** — "VCs fund companies through their UNPROFITABLE 'valley of death'
and growth needs **(PE's do not do this)**." `[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]` — a valley
of death curve.

**Slide 63** — PE "acquires the majority/all of the company, often with DEBT
**that the company pays** …sometimes called **FINANCIAL ENGINEERING** when the
returns are based on growth, but debt leverage. Since there are debt payments to
be made, this is the domain of PROFITABLE COMPANIES."

**Slide 62** — "PRIVATE EQUITY DEALS OFTEN USE DEBT. PRIVATE CAPITAL IS DEBT.
……BUT VENTURE DEALS GENERALLY…….DO NOT USE DEBT (til late stage, maybe)."

## Formulas / Metrics

### The four high-level tools (slide 51) — `SOURCE FACT`

> - **IRR** — Internal Rate of Return — "basically the % return to the investor"
> - **MOIC / "Times Money"** — Times Money on Invested Capital — "basically how
>   many times did the money grow"
> - **EBITDA** — Earnings Before Income Tax, Depreciation, and Amortization —
>   "basically what is the Operating business throwing off, or consuming, from
>   operations of the business"
> - **Multiples** — "Times EBITDA" or "Times Revenue"

*(This supplies the course's own plain-language definition of MOIC, which was
missing from the partial extraction.)*

### Other tools (slide 52) — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Cost of Capital** — for debt and equity plays
- **ALPHA** — measure of return enhancement over benchmark (e.g. S&P 500)
- **BETA** — measure of volatility of a stock over benchmark (e.g. S&P 500)

### IRR rules of thumb (slide 53) — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`, titled "MY QUICK RULE of THUMBS USED"

> **A VC FIRM — TO BE IN THE TOP HALF … needs at least 20%+ IRR on the entire fund**
>
> **NO SUFFICIENT RETURNS? — NO MORE FUNDS.** "Many venture firms are one fund
> and done, permanently."
>
> - 2 times your money in 2 years = ~40% IRR
> - 3 times your money in 3 years = ~40% IRR
> - 4 times your money in 4 years = ~40% IRR
> - **5 times your money in 5 years = ~40% IRR ……. "This is what I would model
>   when looking at a deal"**

The 5-year line is the one he says he actually models. The partial extraction
had only 2x/3x/4x — the 5x line and the "this is what I would model" attribution
are new here, and the 20% top-half fund threshold is new as well.

## Professor Frameworks / Heuristics

### The three players, as metaphors (slide 64) — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`

Titled "The Private Market Players — **My Observed Comparisons/Metaphors**":

> **Investment Bankers** DO THE DEALS and IPOs … **are like SALES PEOPLE.**
> They make money on TRANSACTION size and volume. They make money as QUICKLY as
> the deals get transacted — **Predictable.**
>
> **Venture Capitalists** are like **BETTORS THAT SELL THE HOUSE THEY FUNDED.**
> HI RISK, HI REWARD, LONG TERM investors active in the investment.
> They make money SLOWLY but with MASSIVE UPSIDE — **Unpredictable.**
>
> **Private Equity Investors** are **HOUSE FLIPPERS THAT SELL PROFITABLY, EVEN
> WITHOUT APPRECIATION OF SALE PRICE IN SOME CASES.** OPPORTUNISTS that use
> LEVERAGE, and/or Change the Company, and then FLIP deals.

This is the single most interview-usable slide in the deck — it answers "what's
the difference between VC, PE and banking?" in a form that is both memorable and
clearly his own.

## Career Insights

1. **The "Ripped From The Headlines" memo is portable.** Eight reps of a
   professional-format investment reaction memo. Directly reusable as an
   interview answer structure and as a portfolio artifact.
2. **The metaphor slide (64) is an interview answer**, nearly verbatim.
3. **Deal-size table (60)** calibrates what a seed vs VC vs PE check looks like —
   useful for not sounding naive about round sizes.
4. **"90% of successful ventures are acquired, not IPO'd"** reframes exit
   expectations toward M&A, which connects directly to corporate development as
   a target role.
5. **The 20% fund IRR / "one fund and done"** point explains *why* VCs behave as
   they do — fund-level survival pressure, not deal-level greed.

## Key Takeaways

1. Private markets ~$11T, public ~$54T — but **77% of that public market cap
   traces back to VC-backed companies.** The asset class punches far above its size.
2. **VC is a subset of PE but a totally different process.** VC funds unprofitable
   companies through the valley of death; PE buys profitable companies, often
   with debt the company itself repays.
3. **No exit, no return** — private assets have no market price.
4. **~40% IRR is the modelling target**; 20%+ fund IRR is the survival threshold.
5. The course is taught from a practitioner's career, not a textbook, and is
   openly skeptical of both the industry's self-image and popular depictions of it.
