## Barbarians at the Gate — the debrief

**The book** — `SOURCE FACT`: 1989 **Gerald Loeb Award**; #1 NYT bestseller for
over six months; **592 pages**. "Some critics and authors have called it the best
business book ever written." Chicago Tribune: "hard to imagine a better
story...and it's hard to imagine a better account." *"PS. THE BOOK IS EVEN WILDER."*

### KKR's deal structure — `SOURCE FACT`

**Total: $31.4 billion** — "the largest LBO in history at the time"

| Source | Amount | Share |
|---|---|---|
| KKR Equity | **$1.5B** | **5%** |
| Bank Loans | **$14.9B** | **47%** |
| Junk Bonds | **$15.0B** | **48%** |

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| RJR share price before announcement (Oct 1988) | **$55** |
| KKR winning bid per share | **$109** — "nearly double the pre-deal price" |
| **Premium paid** | **98%** |
| Entry EV/EBITDA | **~10x** — "high for a 1988 LBO" (stated elsewhere as **7.7x** at $109/share) |
| KKR's own equity | **5%** — "the remaining 95% was borrowed" |

> **"KKR controlled a $31.4B company by putting up just $1.5B of its own money.
> This extreme leverage is the defining mechanic of an LBO."**

**Debt/equity ratio was ~8+x — "TWICE TODAYS NORM."** Price ~$31B EV, debt
leverage ~$24–26B, equity $3.5B.

`SOURCE FACT` — "INTERESTING: Ross's initial bid was close to today's average.
Deal was done at **7.7x EV/EBITDA at $109 per share**. If it had been done at
Ross Johnson's initial bid of **$75**, that is **5.29x EV/EBITDA**."

### KKR barely used its own money — `SOURCE FACT`

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| **KKR itself (general partner capital)** | **~$75–100M (~0.3%)** |
| **KKR's Limited Partners** | **~$3.4B (13.7%)** |
| Total Equity | ~$3.5B |

"KKR THEMSELVES PUT IN ONLY ABOUT **0.3%** FROM THEIR FUNDS. THEIR LP'S
CO-INVESTED DIRECTLY."

### The double-dip fee structure — `SOURCE FACT`

> "**KKR was essentially double-dipping on fees** in the RJR deal:
> - **The 1% transaction fee** — their customary 1% management fee on the deal
>   itself, equaling **$200 million on a $20 billion transaction**
> - **AND the fund-level 2 and 20** — as General Partner, also compensated
>   through 2% management fees on committed capital and 20% carried interest
>   paid by the LPs
>
> This double-fee structure became a major point of criticism and was one of the
> practices that later led to greater scrutiny and reform of private equity fee
> arrangements industry-wide.
>
> **In total, KKR received nearly $1B in FEES, irrespective of the deal returns.
> That's the main reason they ended up with a 14% IRR despite the company
> getting sold in pieces at partial value.**"

**Fees are charged on the TOTAL deal size — debt included** — `SOURCE FACT`:
"For a $30 million investment with $10 million in equity and $20 million in debt,
a 1% management fee would be **$300,000** ($30M × 1%)."

### Systemic LBO incentives — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`

> "**SYSTEMIC LBO INCENTIVES ……. COULD PUSH TOO FAR**
> - **FEES — TIED TO DEAL SIZE …….. REWARDS LARGER DEALS**
> - **INTEREST ON LBO DEBT IS TAX-DEDUCTIBLE** (company incurred)
> - **LOWERS WACC** of taking on debt component in financing — easier to do more debt
> - **CREATES A 'TAX SHIELD'** that reduces the company's overall taxable income"

**This is the course's sharpest structural critique**: the fee structure rewards
size, and the tax code subsidizes debt. Both push toward more leverage than is
prudent — a mechanism, not a moral complaint.

### The bankruptcy statistic — `SOURCE FACT`

> "**LBO RISK — 10x HIGHER BANKRUPTCY @ 10 YR.** Research from California
> Polytechnic State University shows that **~20 percent of large LBOs go bankrupt
> within 10 years**, while **matched control firms (normal companies) experience
> a bankruptcy rate of just 2 percent**."

### What happened to RJR — `SOURCE FACT`

"CASH FLOWS NOT ENOUGH from existing ops to pay down the debt (**8x WAS TOO
MUCH**…). So….**DIVESTITURES.** Sale of assets started to pay down debt within a
year after the LBO… eventually included the tobacco division, Nabisco …**SOLD
FOR PARTS.** In the end **only ~$9B of the ~$23B+ debt was ever paid down.** So
the LEVER that AMPLIFIES returns didn't even get halfway there….. **BUT KKR
still made money…..from huge Deal Lead Fees.** It did not work….but don't cry
for KKR….."

> board to 'trust' the expert.**"
>
> "PE firms look to TRIM costs and FLIP it with better financials. Johnson's

## Are LBOs still happening?

`SOURCE FACT` — "AFTER RJR: LESS VALUE, LESS DEBT, LESS DEALS…. **BUT IT CAME
BACK AGAIN…BRIEFLY….**"

**2007 LBO peak — prime contributors:** debt was cheap · junk bonds flourished ·
"Funds were sitting on cash waiting out the post-2000… funds getting long in the
tooth…. eager to put capital to work…**too eager?**"

**LBO market influences** — `SOURCE FACT`: low interest rates ("It determines how
big the LEVERAGE is") · **WACC cycles** · "**THE BIGGER THE LEVERAGE, THE HIGHER
THE RETURNS…theoretically**" · fund cycle situations — "eagerness, excess funds
**'dry powder'** in PE firms and funds."

**Answer:** "**YES BUT….THAT WAS THE PEAK OF THE PEAK IN '89. NEVER AGAIN AT THE
SAME LEVEL, NOR WITH THAT MUCH DEBT….**" And: "WACC MATTERS — BUT ITS NOT THE
ONLY DRIVER IN PE — EXITS AND MARKET CONDITIONS ALSO FACTOR. **LBO DEAL VOLUME
IS CYCLICAL.**"

### Today's market — `SOURCE FACT`
- **~8,500 PE deals last year; ~700 a year are LBOs**
- **Typically bought at 11x EBITDA multiple**
- **~2x debt/equity ratio average (50% equity / 50% debt)** — versus RJR's ~8x
- "This may affect the STRATEGY strongly….**Tighter Squeezing and/or Faster
  Growth**"

### Junk / high-yield bonds today — `SOURCE FACT`
"**~5% HIGHER THAN QUALITY BONDS** (like Treasury bonds). **BONDS FOR PE DEBT —
RANGE HAS BEEN ~7–12% LAST 20 YEARS.** Junk bonds trend like top investment grade
bonds, but carry a higher default rate, and hence a higher interest rate to
overcome the risk to investors."

## Why do an LBO at all? — and the company's alternative

`SOURCE FACT` — "**WHY? COMPANIES FEEL UNDERVALUED…. AND DO LBO TO 'UNLOCK' THE
VALUE (POSSIBLY).**" And candidly: "In some cases….**the company probably
shouldn't!**...maybe….."

### The alternative the course puts forward — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`

> "**COMPANY ALTERNATIVE — PAY IT OUT!** High cash flow companies could pay
> higher dividends with the cash.
> - **Maximizes Shareholder Value** — if generating excess cash with no
>   high-return projects to reinvest in, return cash via dividends or buybacks
> - **It Pre-empts PE** — "PE firms in an LBO use the company's own cash flows
>   (and leverage) to deliver value to their investors. Companies [that] are more
>   aggressive about capital returns [are] less attractive for LBO"
> - **It Avoids Unnecessary Control Shifts** — "an LBO hands control to a PE firm"

### So why don't companies do this? — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Management Incentives** — **"Holding cash = more control = more
  empire-building."**
- **Market Signaling** — "Raising dividends signals a permanent shift in cash
  flow expectations. Companies are cautious because **cutting dividends later is
  a big negative signal.**"
- **Risk Aversion** — "Keeping a large cash cushion makes management feel safe —
  especially in cyclical industries or uncertain markets…….**But are they safe?**"

**This is a genuinely sophisticated framing**: an LBO target is often a company
whose own management created the vulnerability by hoarding cash instead of
returning it.

## Loss rates: VC vs PE — `SOURCE FACT`

> **"VC ~75% do NOT return the investment (1.0 MOIC)"**
>
> "In PE, during normal years, roughly **10–20% of individual deals lose money**.
> But during recessions it spikes dramatically:
> - **2008: 32%** of exited deals lost money
> - **2009: 47%**
> - **2020: 27%**"

**One of the most useful pairs of statistics in the course** — it quantifies the
structural difference between the two models: VC expects most deals to fail and
relies on outliers; PE expects most deals to work and is hurt badly by cycles.

## THE MODEL DEAL — Vista Equity Partners × Marketo

`SOURCE FACT` — "**A GOOD PE Case Study**" / "Marketo — A Vista Partners Private
Equity Success Story." Marketing automation software. Acquired **May 2016**,
sold to **Adobe September 2018**.

| | |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | **$1.79B** (62% premium to prior stock price) |
| Exit price | **$4.75B** |
| Equity MOIC | **~3.1x** |
| Estimated IRR | **~72%** |
| Hold period | **25 months** |

### Why Vista saw an opportunity — `SOURCE FACT`

Revenue ($M): 2012 **58** → 2013 **96** → 2014 **150** → 2015 **210** →
2016 **241** → 2017 **280** → 2018 **321**
YoY growth: 2013 **64%** → 2014 **57%** → 2015 **40%** → 2016 **15%** →
2017 **16%** → 2018 **15%**

> "Growth decelerated from **64% → 15%** before Vista acquired — classic **'value
> unlock' opportunity**: operational improvement to restore growth."

### Capital structure — the key contrast — `SOURCE FACT`

| Deal | Debt share |
|---|---|
| **Marketo (this deal)** | **22%** — "Low leverage" |
| Toys "R" Us LBO | **~85%** — "High leverage — went bankrupt" |
| RJR Nabisco LBO | **~90%** — "High leverage — nearly bankrupt" |

Equity $1,390M (78%) / Debt $400M (22%).
> "**Vista focused on operations and growth — not financial engineering.** Low
> leverage = less risk, more upside from business improvement."

### The turnaround — `SOURCE FACT`

| | At acquisition (mid-2016) | Mid-ownership (2017) | At exit (mid-2018) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $241M | $280M | $321M |
| **Net margin** | **−31%** | **−10%** | **+2%** |

> "Vista traded **'growth at any cost' (60¢ of every dollar on sales &
> marketing)** for disciplined profitable growth — the core of their **operator
> playbook**."

### Timeline — `SOURCE FACT`
- **May 2016** — Vista acquires, $1.79B, 62% premium
- **Oct 2016** — **New CEO installed (Steve Lucas)**; "Operator playbook begins"
- **2017** — Product & cloud migration; Gartner leader (6th year); Google Cloud partnership
- **Early 2018** — Revenue $321M, profitable; margin turns positive from −31%
- **Sep 2018** — Adobe acquires at $4.75B, **14.8x revenue — 3rd highest ever**
  for a large software deal. "Vista's largest exit at the time."

### The three value levers — `SOURCE FACT`

| Driver | Contribution | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue growth / EBITDA expansion** | **~45%** | $241M → $321M; "The core business got healthier." |
| **Multiple expansion — strategic buyer premium** | **~35%** | "Bought at ~7.4x revenue. Sold at 14.8x revenue. **Adobe paid a massive strategic premium to beat Salesforce** in the enterprise marketing automation war." |
| **Margin improvement** | **~20%** | −31% to positive. "Cutting bloated S&M spend while maintaining growth is Vista's signature move." |

Value waterfall ($M): entry **1,790** + revenue growth **1,300** + multiple
expansion **1,110** + margin improvement **550** = exit **4,750**.
*(Deck notes the splits are illustrative estimates.)*

### MOIC in context — `SOURCE FACT`
| Benchmark | MOIC |
|---|---|
| PE deals that lost money | 0.7x |
| **PE industry average** | **2.3x** |
| Top-quartile PE | 3.0x |
| **Vista / Marketo** | **3.1x** |
| Exceptional PE (rare) | 5.0x |

"Vista's ~3.1x MOIC significantly beats the PE industry average of 2.0–2.5x. In
just 25 months — roughly 1/3 of a typical 5–7 year hold period." IRR ~72% versus
"the PE industry average of ~14%."

### The four takeaways — `SOURCE FACT`
1. **Low leverage was the foundation** — 22% vs 80–90% in disastrous deals
2. **Operational improvement, not financial engineering** — replaced leadership,
   cut bloated S&M, migrated to Google Cloud, turned it profitable
3. **Strategic exit timing was critical** — "Adobe desperately needed to compete
   with Salesforce"
4. **~3.1x MOIC in 25 months = exceptional**

> **"The best PE deals grow the business AND time the exit right — this one did both."**

**This is the deliberate mirror of RJR Nabisco.** Same asset class, opposite
method: minimal leverage, operational work, a strategic buyer who needed the
asset. It is the course's answer to what "good PE" looks like — and the single
best PE interview example available here.

## Private Credit

`SOURCE FACT` — "**Private credit = loans made by non-bank investors directly to
companies, bypassing Wall Street.** Think of it as: banks stepped back after
2008, and investment funds stepped in to do the lending instead."

| | |
|---|---|
| Market size 2024 | **$2 Trillion** |
| Projected AUM by 2030 | **~$4.5T** |
| **Share of LBO debt in 2024 from private funds** | **77%** |

*Sources cited on the slide: Dechert LLP, S&P Global, BlackRock, Arcesium 2025.*

### Where it came from — a 40-year story — `SOURCE FACT`
- **1970s–80s — Junk Bond Era.** "Michael Milken at Drexel Burnham Lambert
  pioneers high-yield debt to fund leveraged buyouts. **Non-bank lending is born.**"
- **1980 BDC Act.** "Congress creates BDCs to channel private investment into
  small & mid-sized US companies — the first legal framework for private lending funds."
- **2008 Crisis — Banks Pull Back.** "Post-crisis regulations (**Dodd-Frank,
  Basel III**) force banks to hold more capital and shed risky loans. **A massive
  lending vacuum opens up.**"
- **2010–Today — Private Credit Explodes.** "AUM grows from **$500B (2014) to $2T
  (2024)** — a 4x increase in 10 years."

### Who does the lending — `SOURCE FACT`
- **PE firms with credit arms** — Blackstone Credit, KKR Credit, Apollo.
  "**Apollo's private credit AUM hit $723B. Credit is now bigger than PE at some
  firms.**"
- **Specialty credit managers** — Ares, HPS, Blue Owl, Golub. "**Ares Capital
  (ARCC) is the world's largest BDC with $22B+ in assets.**"
- **Business Development Companies** — ARCC, FS KKR, Prospect Capital. "Publicly
  traded vehicles that lend to small/mid companies… **Required to distribute
  90%+ of income.**"
- **Insurance companies** — Athene (Apollo), Global Atlantic. "Insurers love
  private credit's stable income — **it matches their long-term liabilities.**"
- **Pension & sovereign funds** — CalPERS, CPP, Singapore GIC. "**Over 150 US
  allocators joined since 2020.**"

> "Key takeaway: **PE firms often have credit arms, but they are separate
> businesses. PE buys equity; private credit makes loans.**"

### How a deal works — `SOURCE FACT`
```
Institutional Investors → Private Credit Fund → Direct Loan → Borrower
(pensions, insurers,      (Ares, Blackstone,    (senior secured,  (mid-market,
 endowments)               Apollo, Blue Owl)     floating rate)    $50M–$500M rev,
                                                                   PE-backed)
                         → Returns: interest income + fees, 9–12% yield
```

**Typical loan terms:**
| Term | Value |
|---|---|
| Loan size | $100M–$2B+ (growing) |
| Rate | **SOFR + 500–650 bps (~10–12% total)** |
| Structure | Senior secured, first lien (safest claim) |
| Tenor | 5–7 years |
| Covenants | Maintenance covenants (quarterly tests) |
| Liquidity | **Illiquid — no public market to sell** |

### The five types — risk/return pyramid — `SOURCE FACT`

| Type | Return | Risk | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distressed Debt** | 15–25%+ | Very High | "Buy loans/bonds of struggling companies below par. Profit on recovery or restructuring." |
| **Mezzanine / Subordinated** | 12–18% | High | "Junior to senior loans. Unsecured but may have **equity warrants ('kickers')**." |
| **Unitranche / One-Stop** | 10–13% | Medium | "A single combined first/second lien loan from one fund. **Very common in mid-market LBOs.**" |
| **Direct Lending (First Lien)** | 9–12% | Medium-Low | "**The LARGEST private credit category (~$1.3T of the $2T market).**" |
| **Asset-Based Finance** | 7–10% | Low | "Backed by hard assets: mortgages, auto loans, aircraft leases, receivables." |

Composition: "Majority (~60%+) standalone private credit fund managers with
institutional capital. But a large minority, **30–40%, is lending to PE-owned
companies** — and about half of that is lending from traditional buyout PE funds."

### Ten takeaways — selected `SOURCE FACT`
- **"It's NOT junk bonds"** — "Private credit is a **bilateral loan — illiquid,
  floating rate, heavily covenanted.** Junk bonds are **public, fixed-rate,
  tradeable.**"
- **Returns over 10 years:** private credit **~10%** · PE **~15%** · VC **~11.5%
  median** (but top VC can be 25%+)
- **"You give up liquidity"** — investors earn an **illiquidity premium of 3–5%**
  above equivalent public debt
- **"It's not risk-free"** — "**Default rates are rising (2.5% in Q4 2025).**
  Underwriting discipline varies. **Manager selection matters — a lot.**"

## Key Takeaways

1. **KKR controlled a $31.4B company with $1.5B of equity — and only ~0.3% of
   that was KKR's own money.**
2. **Fees are charged on total deal size including debt, and stack with 2-and-20**
   — KKR earned ~$1B in fees regardless of the outcome, which is why they still
   cleared 14% IRR on a deal that was sold for parts.
3. **The incentive structure pushes toward more leverage**: fees reward size, and
   interest is tax-deductible, creating a shield that lowers WACC.
4. **~20% of large LBOs go bankrupt within 10 years vs 2% for comparable
   companies.**
5. **~75% of VC deals fail to return capital; PE loses money on 10–20% of deals
   in normal years, 47% in 2009.**
6. **Vista/Marketo is what good PE looks like** — 22% leverage, operational fix,
   3.1x in 25 months, strategic buyer paying 14.8x revenue.
7. **A company can pre-empt an LBO by returning cash** — most don't, because
   holding cash means control.
8. **Private credit now funds 77% of LBO debt** — a $2T market created by
   post-2008 bank regulation.
9. **PE-owned insurers investing in PE-originated private credit is a live
   conflict-of-interest issue — and it is happening in Iowa.**
