## Part 2 — Do VCs add value beyond money?

`SOURCE FACT` — **"Do VC's 'add value' beyond money? ~its about 50/50…."**

| Value-added activity | % of founders reporting benefit | Evidence of impact |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic guidance / mentoring | 60–70% | Better CEO selection, pivot decisions |
| Network & introductions | 50–65% | Faster customer acquisition, follow-on funding |
| Recruiting / talent support | 40–50% | Faster senior hires |
| Governance & oversight | 60% | Fewer management mistakes, improved scaling |
| **Pressure / conflicts** | **30–40%** | **Short-term exit pressure, diluted control** |

**Note the last row is a cost, not a benefit** — the table is deliberately
two-sided, consistent with the course's "…or not….." skepticism.

### VCs leave boards at exit — `SOURCE FACT`
> "VC's usually leave boards at investment exit. Once they have exited an
> investment, it's hard to argue that they should be spending their time on a
> public board. **They should be doing more deals in their fund or raising a new
> one.** Boards in the same market as other investments can be problematic too.
> **Ben Rosen's staying for 18 years after IPO is very rare.**"

### Founder-CEO survival — `SOURCE FACT`
A study of **over 18,000 VC-backed firms funded between 1990 and 2012** found:
- **Founder was CEO at IPO only 41% of the time**
- **Three years into being public, down another 20%**
- "CEO management changes are the norm — by IPO"

**This is a strong interview statistic** — it quantifies the founder-replacement
dynamic that `SRC-P-005`'s protective provisions and board rights enable.

## Part 3 — Valuations and Term Sheets

### The core claim — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`
> **"THE COMPANY is RE-VALUED (somewhat arbitrarily) EVERY FINANCING ROUND**
> and OFTEN OWNERSHIP CHANGES; BOARD/MANAGEMENT CHANGES often happen at each
> financing."

### Who prices what — `SOURCE FACT`
1. **VC rounds** — priced by VCs in their term sheets, up to IPO
2. **Then the IPO** — priced by investment bankers

### What the price is based on — `SOURCE FACT`
> "It is based on things factual and not…."
> - the dynamics of the company, and **AVERAGE ROUND VALUES by space**
> - market conditions — "especially the IPO estimated valuations"
> - **the timeline of the investment funds**
> - other terms like Liquidation Preferences

### What is NOT used — `SOURCE FACT`, important
> **"Valuation Metrics like Discounted Cash Flow are NOT used, there is often no
> cash flow!"**
>
> **"Market averages by Round are the most common valuation method for rounds."**
> Tracked and posted by the **National Venture Capital Association**; "Others
> such as **Pitchbook** do as well."

**This is one of the most useful corrections in the course.** A finance graduate
arrives expecting DCF; the course states plainly that early-stage venture
pricing is comparables-driven because there are no cash flows to discount.

### The formulas — `SOURCE FACT`
```
PRE money  = value of the company before an investment
POST money = value after the investment

PRE + MONEY = POST
MONEY / POST = OWNERSHIP BOUGHT = DILUTION TO FOUNDERS
```

**Worked example** — "typical Shark Tank like Seed Round":
> "$250,000 for 50% of your company"
> $250,000 / 50% ownership = **$500,000 Post-Money Valuation**
> → **$250,000 Pre-Money Valuation**

"Setting the Pre-Money Valuation establishes how much Investor gets in the
company."

## Part 4 — Initial Public Offerings

### What an IPO is — `SOURCE FACT`
First sale of stock to the public; transition from private to public; raises
capital for growth; **creates liquidity for early investors**.

Other benefits: "Brand visibility and prestige and stability. Could be very
important for a **Large Customer** — they are often concerned about a company's
ability to serve them, and to access the capital to do so."

### History — `SOURCE FACT`
- **First IPO: Dutch East India Company, 1602** — "a 423 year old concept."
  Formed by uniting six pre-companies, capital of **6.4 million guilders**, with
  authority to monopolize Dutch trade in Asia. "It went public to raise the
  massive capital needed for its long and dangerous voyages to Asia."
- US IPO market growth post-WWII → tech boom of the 1990s → **SPAC & Direct
  Listing era** as the recent variation

### Exchange sizes — `SOURCE FACT`
| Exchange | Market cap of all listed companies |
|---|---|
| NYSE | **$32T** |
| Nasdaq | **$30T** |
| London Stock Exchange | **$6T** |
| Hong Kong Exchange | **$6T** |

### Process and timeline — `SOURCE FACT`
**The IPO process takes 18–24 months.** Steps: decision to go public → selecting
underwriters/investment bankers → filing and SEC review → pricing, allocation,
trading debut.

The startup-to-IPO timeline slide: **VC or PE fund life 10 years** · deal find,
due diligence, close **0.5 year** · company funded → high growth → prep for IPO
→ investment banker process → **lockup**.

### Role of investment banks — `SOURCE FACT`
Advise on timing and pricing · market the IPO (roadshow) · underwrite shares
(buy then resell) · provide after-market support.

> "The investment bank acts as **intermediary, advisor, and risk-taker**. They
> buy shares from the company at a discount and resell them to investors,
> earning the spread (**typically 5–7%**) as compensation. In return, they assume
> the risk that shares won't sell."

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lead Underwriter** | Main bank coordinating the process |
| **Syndicate** | Group of banks sharing risk — **"CALIX USED 4 BANKS"** |
| **Spread** | **5–7%** discount earned by the banks |
| **Greenshoe** | Right to sell up to **15% more shares** — provides flexibility for demand, helps stabilize price, common in US IPOs |

### SEC and regulation — `SOURCE FACT`
Focus: **"PROTECTING INVESTORS BY ASSURING COMPLETE and EQUAL DISSEMINATION OF
INFORMATION."** Securities Act of 1933 · disclosure requirements (S-1) ·
JOBS Act 2012+.

**The S-1 (also called a prospectus)** — "comprehensive disclosure of
everything": company history & business model · financial statements ·
**capitalization table** · **stock option pool** · risks & uncertainties · use of
proceeds. "Companies that intentionally leave out required information (or
material risks) can face litigation."

**Public vs confidential filing** — `SOURCE FACT`:
- Traditional: **S-1 immediately public when filed — even if draft.** Anyone can
  view it on **EDGAR**.
- **Confidential filing (most common today)**: allowed for companies with **less
  than $1B annual revenue** ("emerging growth companies," EGCs). Filed
  confidentially, but **must be made public 15–21 days before the roadshow
  begins.** "Gives companies privacy to test market conditions and withdraw
  quietly if needed."

### The roadshow — `SOURCE FACT`
~**2 weeks**, at the very end of a much longer process. CEO and CFO travel to
major financial centers — **New York, Boston, San Francisco, Los Angeles**,
sometimes London. *"THIS IS ONE REASON WHY INVESTMENT BANKS ARE IN THESE
LOCATIONS."* Multiple back-to-back presentations daily with mutual funds, hedge
funds, pension funds.

Goals: generate excitement, explain the business story, answer tough questions,
**gauge investor demand to help set the final price**. "These meetings help
underwriters build the **'book'** of orders."

**Timing:** after the roadshow wraps, the IPO is priced **within 24–48 hours**,
and **the stock begins trading the very next morning.**

### Pricing — `SOURCE FACT`, four steps

1. **Initial Valuation** — comparable companies, precedent transactions, DCF
   models, growth analysis *(note: DCF appears here, at IPO, where it was
   explicitly ruled out for venture rounds)*
2. **Set Initial Price Range** — a conservative range in the S-1 (like **$18–$22**),
   **typically 15–20% wide** to allow upward revision
3. **Roadshow & Book Building** — collect orders, measure demand, calculate
   oversubscription
4. **Final Pricing** — the evening before trading, based on the order book

**Oversubscription benchmarks** — `SOURCE FACT`:
| Ratio | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Under 1x** | **Major red flag** |
| 3–5x | Healthy |
| **5x+** | Typically prices **10–20% above range** |
| 10x+ | Very strong |

"NOTE: **INVESTMENT BANKS WANT a 5–10X OVERSUBSCRIPTION IDEALLY**" (also stated
as "10x the Allotment Ideally").

### The "Pop" strategy — `SOURCE FACT`
> "Explains why bankers **intentionally underprice by 10–25%** to create positive
> first-day momentum, though **excessive pops (50%+) suggest the company left
> money on the table.**"

**A genuinely important idea for interviews** — the first-day pop is not an
accident or pure market enthusiasm; it is engineered, and too large a pop is a
failure of pricing from the issuer's perspective.

### Lock-up periods — `SOURCE FACT`
Typically **90–180 days** (elsewhere "usually 180"). Restriction on insider
selling; prevents oversupply; **expiration has an impact on stock price.**

### Allocation priority — `SOURCE FACT`
1. **Institutional investors** — "many will be long term holders"
2. **Retail investors** — limited access
3. **Friends & family allocations**

### IPO Readiness — The Rule of 40 — `SOURCE FACT`
> "**Revenue growth rate (%) + profit margin (%) ≥ 40%** indicates a healthy SaaS
> company."
>
> "For 'profit margin' in startups it is usually **Free Cash Flow or EBITDA**
> …closest to actual cash generated/consumed by the business….rather than
> **GAAP Net Income** (which has non-cash items in it like depreciation etc)."

"Bankers, venture investors, and IPO analysts often visualize the Rule of 40 as
charts showing the trade-off between revenue growth and profitability."
`[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]` — "Rule of 40 Examples — Assessing IPO Readiness."

### IPO pros and cons — `SOURCE FACT`

**Pros:** usually produces the highest return · **eliminates Preferred Stock (and
its Protective Provisions) — like Liquidation Preferences** · raises significant
capital and allows investors an exit · reduces the cost of future capital
("secondary offerings can follow easily") · increased reputation and recognition
· can use stock to attract talent and compensate employees.

**Cons:** long, complex, high underwriter and attorney costs · regulatory,
filing and reporting requirements · **mandatory transparent finances — regulator
and shareholder scrutiny is permanent** · lock-out period restricts when founders
and employees can sell.

"It is expensive to maintain public filings compliance." `[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]`
— six consecutive slides titled "Public company requirements and annual cost"
carry the actual dollar figures. **A substantial gap.**

### Shelving vs withdrawing — `SOURCE FACT`
- **"Shelving"** = putting the IPO on hold indefinitely. Reasons: poor market
  conditions, valuation isn't where you want it, need time to improve financials.
  "The S-1 stays on file but goes stale, and the company can revive it later."
- **"Withdrawing"** = the formal legal action terminating that specific IPO
  attempt. "More definitive, though it doesn't prevent filing a new S-1 later."

## Key Takeaways

1. **DCF is not used to price venture rounds** — there is often no cash flow.
   Market averages by round (NVCA, PitchBook) are the standard method.
2. **PRE + MONEY = POST; MONEY / POST = ownership bought = founder dilution.**
3. **Companies are re-valued somewhat arbitrarily every round**, and board and
   management changes often come with each financing.
4. **VC value-add is roughly 50/50**, and the same table lists pressure and
   diluted control as a real cost.
5. **Only 41% of founders are still CEO at IPO.**
6. **The IPO pop is engineered** — bankers underprice 10–25% deliberately.
7. **Rule of 40** is the IPO-readiness screen, using FCF/EBITDA not GAAP net income.
8. **An IPO wipes out preferred stock and its liquidation preferences** — which
   is why it is the outcome where preferences stop mattering (see `SRC-P-005`).
