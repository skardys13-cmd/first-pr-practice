## HEDGE FUNDS

### What a hedge fund is — `SOURCE FACT`
- A **private pooled fund** — from mostly institutions + wealthy investors
- **Actively managed, typically aiming for absolute returns** — often higher risk
- Can trade many asset classes and instruments — **public AND private**
- Flexible strategies: **shorting, leverage, derivatives, concentrated bets**
- **Less regulated than mutual funds — if regulated at all. "A bit of nearly
  ANYTHING GOES"**

### The fee structure — and how it differs — `SOURCE FACT`
> "**2% and 20% but the fees are dynamic and tied to 2% of Net Asset Value ….that
> is Marked to Market every quarter**…..and then some paid out.
>
> The 2% fee is of the **Net Asset Value of the Fund**. So **if the fund goes up,
> so do your fees….and vice versa. DIFFERENT [from] 2% in PE and VC.**"

**A precise and important distinction:** PE/VC charge 2% on *committed capital*;
hedge funds charge on *NAV*, marked to market quarterly. Hedge fund managers'
fee income rises and falls with performance; PE/VC management fees do not.

### Regulation — `SOURCE FACT`
- "The SEC only requires hedge funds to register if they have **more than $150
  million** in private funds"
- "Only funds with **AUM of $500 million or more** must file quarterly and report
  the details of their liabilities and assets"
- "Form PF… used for confidential event reporting"
- > **"So a $100M Hedge fund is unregistered, unregulated, and has no reporting
  > obligations"**

### Who can invest — `SOURCE FACT`
"Hedge funds are set up to **NOT allow small public investors.** Higher minimum
investment requirements, only accept **accredited investors ($250K+ salary, $1M+
net worth)**. Often have high minimum investment amounts (**$1M+**). **This is why
hedge funds are sometimes considered to be a luxury for the rich…..**"

**Minimums:** $1–5M+ institutional · $1M+ individual · **fund of funds
$100,000–250,000 "but with additional fees"** · trend toward higher minimums for
top-performing funds. Legal requirements: accredited investor status; **qualified
purchaser status** for certain funds.

### History — `SOURCE FACT`
| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1949** | **First hedge fund — Alfred Winslow Jones** |
| 1970s–80s | Gradual industry growth |
| 1990s | Expansion, diversification of strategies |
| 2000s | Mainstream institutional acceptance |
| 2008 | Challenges during financial crisis |
| 2010s– | "Recovery? and continued evolution?" |

**Jones's innovation** — `SOURCE FACT`: "the first money manager to combine
**SHORT SELLING** (making money when the equity falls in value), **LEVERAGE**,
and **POOLING INVESTMENTS** [from] other investors. This investing innovation is
now called **the classic long/short model.**"

> **"In 1952, he altered the structure of his investment vehicle to be the first
> modern PE/VC/Hedge fund structure — a LP/GP, 2% fees, 20% carried interest
> structure."**

**A genuinely notable historical claim: the entire 2-and-20 LP/GP structure used
by VC and PE today originates with a 1952 hedge fund.**

### Long and short — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Long position** — "you buy a stock expecting its price to go up, so you can
  sell later at a profit"
- **Short sale** — "sale of a stock **borrowed for a fee and time slot**, then
  immediately sold, then repurchased ….hopefully at a lower price…then
  returned." "**Short selling allows investors TO PROFIT FROM FALLING STOCK
  PRICES.**" "**Short selling carries unlimited loss potential**" — "there is no
  limit to how high a stock price can rise"

**Other reasons to short** — `SOURCE FACT`: speculation · **hedging and risk
management** ("hold a long position in one stock while shorting a competitor to
reduce industry-wide risk") · **overpriced discovery** ("short sellers identify
overpriced stocks, which can bring stock prices to a more reasonable market
value") · **targeting struggling companies** ("shorting is used against **'zombie
companies'** considered to be dying/significantly overvalued")

### They don't actually hedge anymore — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`
> "**HEDGING — IS BUYING POSITIVE AND NEGATIVELY CORRELATED ASSETS TO REDUCE
> RISK. BUT TODAY…….THEY DON'T HEDGE MUCH……**
>
> Morphed — **from Hedging as risk-reduction → to return-seeking across many
> strategies.** A true 'hedge' would invest in things positively and also
> negatively correlated with the market….**not actually very common.**
>
> Today…..**'Hedge fund' often describes the structure + fees + redemptions
> etc……. more than true hedging.**"

Nomenclature to know: **subscriptions, lockups, mark to market, NAV,
redemptions.** "Liquidity is partially available….but variable. Complex."

### Arbitrage — `SOURCE FACT`
"Exploiting a price difference for the same (or very similar) asset in different
places or forms — buying where cheap and simultaneously selling where expensive."

> **Basic example:** "Imagine a used car is selling for **$10,000 in rural Iowa
> and $12,000 in Chicago.** If you can buy it, truck it, and sell it for a net
> profit — that's arbitrage. Financial markets work the same way, just at
> lightning speed."

- **Pure/Classic Arbitrage** — "**almost extinct today because computers close
  these gaps in milliseconds**"
- **Merger Arbitrage (Risk Arb)** — "When Company A announces it will buy Company
  B at **$50/share**, Company B's stock might only trade at **$47** because
  there's a chance the deal falls apart. **You buy at $47 hoping to collect $50
  at closing.**"

**Why it matters** — `SOURCE FACT`:
> "**This is exactly what died post-2008.** Hedge funds built their pre-2008
> alpha largely on arbitrage strategies — merger arb, convertible arb, fixed
> income arb. **As the industry flooded with capital, too many funds chased the
> same gaps, which compressed the spreads to near zero.**"

### M&A arbitrage in detail — `SOURCE FACT`
"M&A of public entities creates two prices. **Deals must be announced to the
public when signed…NOT closed.** Offer price (future) — revealed in the deal
terms. Market price (today) — investors factor in the chance it won't close.
**Gap = arbitrage opportunity.**"

**Why the gap exists:** deal might fail · time delay (months to close) · **due
diligence findings/failures** · **regulatory uncertainty — DOJ could block, or
require a divestiture** · financing uncertainty · investor demands, consent issues

### CASE — AT&T / T-Mobile 2011 deal failure — `SOURCE FACT`
| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **March 20, 2011** | AT&T announces intent to acquire T-Mobile for **~$39B** |
| **August 31, 2011** | **DOJ files antitrust lawsuit to block** — "reduced competition, higher prices" |
| **November 2011** | **FCC moves toward administrative hearing** — "a second major regulatory rejection" |
| **December 19, 2011** | **Deal abandoned** |

> **Breakup fee paid: total ≈ $4 billion — ~$3B cash + ~$1B spectrum** and
> additional considerations

*(Complements the Qualcomm/NXP $2B example in `SRC-P-012`.)*

## HEDGE FUND PERFORMANCE — the controversy

`SOURCE FACT` — **"SINCE 2012…. HEDGE FUNDS HAVE PERFORMED WORSE …THAN THE
BENCHMARK S&P500/INDEX FUND"**

- **Alpha turned negative after 2009** — "alpha ~3.4% earlier, **~−1.0%
  beginning 2009 (2009–2019)**." *Source cited: EvidenceInvestor summary of
  Rodney Sullivan, Journal of Alternative Investments.*
- **2023–2025: "NEGATIVE ALPHA of 12–16!"** (12–16 percentage points below
  benchmark) in big up years
- **"S&P wins 13/16 years vs average HF; HF wins only 2015, 2018, 2022"**
  (2010–2025). *Source: RIABiz.*
- **2025 snapshot:** HFRI FWC **+12.52%** vs S&P 500 total return **+17.88%**
- On crypto: hedge fund digital asset exposure rose **37% (2022) → 29% (2023) →
  47% (2024)** *(PwC + AIMA)*, but **"Crypto Didn't Magically Create the 'Crypto
  level Alpha' Either"** — VisionTrack Composite ~**40% in 2024 vs Bitcoin
  ~120%**

> Honest methodological caveat on the slide: "**This deck shows 'benchmark
> underperformance' clearly. A full 'alpha' model requires monthly returns +
> factor regressions.**"

### Why did it break? — `SOURCE FACT`
> "The industry grew from **$200 billion at the turn of the millennium to nearly
> $6 trillion today. That is a 30x INCREASE.**
>
> **The strategies simply cannot absorb that much capital without collapsing
> their own returns.** Too much money to put to work on a limited set of
> mispricings, opportunities, arbitrage etc."

**This is the course's core explanation of capacity constraints in investing** —
and it applies well beyond hedge funds. Compare `SRC-P-012`'s $3.7T of PE dry
powder chasing the same assets.

### Strategies — `SOURCE FACT`

making** · M&A arbitrage · arbitrage of pricing/currency/commodities · crypto

## REAL ESTATE — REITs and PE Real Estate

`SOURCE FACT` — "Combines elements of private equity discipline with the unique
characteristics of property investments."

### LP allocations — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Public Pension Funds: 8–12% of assets** — "**more than VC ~= 7%**"
- Corporate Pension Plans: 5–10% of assets

"Higher returns = more risk + less liquidity. **Public REITs offer the best
liquidity but share market volatility. PE opportunistic funds target the highest
returns but lock up capital for a decade.**"

### What a REIT is — `SOURCE FACT`
- "Company pools investor money — **just like a mutual fund, but for real estate**"
- Buys income-producing properties: **offices, malls, apartments, warehouses,
  hospitals**
- Collects rent — "steady cash flow from long-term leases"
- **"90% of taxable income must be paid out as dividends — by law"** (IRS)
- **"Must have 75%+ of assets in real estate"** — IRS qualification rule
- **"Avoids double taxation"** — "The REIT is not taxed that way…..but the
  recipient of the dividends is….but no 'Double Taxation' of both REIT and the
  Dividend Recipient"

> "In a way, **the REIT is similar to the PE 'Platform company' idea**"

### Two components of return — `SOURCE FACT`
> "**RE pays Dividends……..PE does not….**
>
> TWO COMPONENTS OF RETURN: **DIVIDENDS AND PRICE APPRECIATION.** Dividends are
> strong in Real Estate. PLUS you get the price appreciation… Dividends are a
> bigger portion of returns than in PE.
>
> ***VERY DIFFERENT THAN TYPICAL PE WHERE THE INCOME IS FROM THE EXIT
> APPRECIATION ONLY***"

### Fund structure — `SOURCE FACT`
"Similar to PE and VC funds — a '2 AND 20'":
- **GP** — investment manager responsible for acquisitions, asset management, strategy
- **LPs** — institutional and accredited investors providing capital
- **Fund Life: usually 7–10 years**
- **Management Fees: typically 1–2%** of committed/invested capital
- **Carried Interest: usually 15–20% of profits after a preferred return hurdle**

### Other RE vehicles — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Direct Property Acquisition** — "PE Buying Real Estate instead of a
  company…" then enhance value through renovations, drive income from leasing,
  implement operational improvements
- **Joint Ventures** — "Collaborations between PE firms and local developers or
  operators. Combining capital with on-the-ground expertise. **Large real estate
  projects tend to involve a 'syndicate' of multiple investors more than typical
  PE deals.**"
- **Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs)** — "When not using a REIT, it is common to
  set up an SPV as the legal entity you invest in. **This creates financial risk
  isolation into the SPV.** Often used for single-property investments."
- **Sale-Leaseback** — "PE firms purchase properties from owners, THEN…..they
  lease them back. This provid[es] liquidity to the seller, and stable returns
  to the investor."
- **Distressed Asset Investments** — "Acquiring underperforming or foreclosed
  properties at discounted prices, aiming for value appreciation through
  repositioning, improvements."

### The "buttons" in RE PE — `SOURCE FACT`
Asset Selection ("Identifying mispriced or undervalued properties") · **Financial
Engineering** ("Optimal debt structures and refinancing") · Operational
Improvements · **Repositioning** ("Physical improvements and tenant upgrades") ·
**Market Timing** ("Entry/exit based on real estate and economic cycles") ·
Development ("Ground-up construction or significant redevelopment")

### Public vs Private REITs — `SOURCE FACT`

| Feature | **Public REIT** | **Private REIT** |
|---|---|---|
| Listed on | NYSE / NASDAQ | **No — sold direct to investors** |
| Liquidity | **High — sell shares any day** | **Low — locked up 5–7+ years** |
| Min. Investment | **~$10 per share** | **$10,000 – $250,000+** |
| Transparency | SEC-regulated, full disclosure | Limited reporting required |
| Who can invest | Anyone with a brokerage | **Accredited investors only** |
| Typical Yield | **3% – 6% dividend yield** | **6% – 10%+ target return** |
| Examples | Prologis, Simon Property, Equinox | **Blackstone BREIT, Starwood SREIT** |

| If you are… | The fit |
|---|---|
| "More of a **SALES PERSON**? Sell with facts, teasers, accretion, synergies…. and do **7+ deals a year**, and paid a % of that value?" | **INVESTMENT BANKING** |

## Key Takeaways

1. **Hedge fund fees are charged on NAV, marked to market quarterly** — unlike
   PE/VC's fee on committed capital.
2. **A $100M hedge fund is entirely unregistered and unregulated.**
3. **The LP/GP 2-and-20 structure originates with Alfred Winslow Jones in 1952** —
   VC and PE inherited it from a hedge fund.
4. **Hedge funds don't really hedge** — the name now describes structure, fees
   and redemption terms.
5. **Arbitrage-driven alpha died post-2008** because capital flooded in and
   compressed spreads — the industry went from $200B to ~$6T, a 30x increase.
6. **The S&P beat the average hedge fund in 13 of 16 years**, and major LPs
   including CalPERS have exited entirely.
7. **REITs must distribute 90% of taxable income and hold 75%+ in real estate**,
   which is what avoids double taxation — and why RE pays dividends while PE
   does not.
8. **Match the role to your temperament** — the closing framework maps attention
   span and risk appetite onto IB, VC, PE, hedge funds, and real estate.
9. **You make upside money by owning something valuable.**
