## The PE Leverage Playbook

`SOURCE FACT` — "A Page from the PE Playbook: Using LEVERAGE"
> "Use **other people's money (OPM debt)** to buy companies..
> … make them pay the Leverage that the PE places on their balance sheet after
> borrowed money to buy them……
> ….then sell at a much higher equity value
> …..**all while putting in very little capital upfront.**"

- **"LEVERAGE is used in ~1 out of 4 deals"**
- **2024 average leverage ratio in buyout deals: 4.5x – 4.7x Debt/EBITDA**

### Financing rates by debt type (dated Apr 26) — `SOURCE FACT`

| Debt type | Rate |
|---|---|
| **Bank Notes / Senior Debt** | 6% – 12% depending on credit quality |
| **High Yield — BB rated** | ~6% |
| **High Yield — B rated** | ~7% – 9% |
| **High Yield — CCC rated** | often exceed 11% |
| **Mezzanine Debt** | Total target return **12% – 20%**; cash coupon 8–12%; **equity warrants/rights adding 2–8%** |
| **Subordinated Debt** | **13% – 20%** "given its junior position in the capital stack" |

### The LBO Capital Stack — `SOURCE FACT`

| Layer | Share | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Senior Secured Bank Debt** | ~50% | ← SAFEST / FIRST PAID IN BANKRUPTCY |
| **Senior Unsecured Notes** | ~20% | |
| **Junk Bonds (Sub. Debt)** | ~25% | |
| **PE Equity** | **~5%** | RISKIEST / LAST PAID → |

> "In an LBO, **junk bonds fill the gap between what banks will lend and what the
> deal costs — at a price.**"
>
> **Why banks won't lend it all:** "Regulators cap how much banks can lend to a
> single highly-leveraged deal. Junk bonds tap institutional investors — **pension
> funds, hedge funds, mutual funds** — who accept higher risk for higher yield."
>
> **The junk bond premium:** "A junk-rated borrower might pay **10–12% annually**
> on its bonds vs. **5–6% for a bank loan.** In an LBO, those interest payments
> come out of company cash flow."
>
> **Milken & Drexel:** "Milken proved in the 1980s that **a diversified portfolio
> of junk bonds yielded more than investment-grade bonds even after defaults.**
> Drexel Burnham became the engine of the LBO boom until its collapse in 1990."
>
> **Callable & covenant-lite:** "Most LBO junk bonds are **callable** — the
> company can repay early if rates fall or EBITDA grows. Modern bonds are
> increasingly **'covenant-lite'**, offering investors fewer protections against
> further debt loading."

## RJR Nabisco — the complete entry-to-exit math

`SOURCE FACT` — additional detail beyond `SRC-P-010`:
- **RJR's EBITDA at time of deal: ~$2.8–3.0 billion**
- **Implied EV/EBITDA: ~10–11x** — "note started at a more normal **5x**"
- Debt used: **~$29.9 billion (roughly 95%)**
- **"KKR eventually took RJR public again in 1991 and sold remaining stake by
  mid-1990s"**
- **"The deal was considered only moderately profitable for KKR due to the very
  high price paid and subsequent tobacco litigation concerns — KKR roughly broke
  even, far below typical LBO returns"**

### The step-by-step numbers — `SOURCE FACT`

| ($B) | Entry — 1989 | Exit — ~1995 est. |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise Value | **31.4** | **26** |
| Net Debt | **29.9** | **18** |
| **Equity Value** | **1.5** | **8** |

```
Step 1: KKR pays $31.4B. Equity in $1.5B, Debt $29.9B. EBITDA at entry ~$3.0B
Step 2: RJR's large cash flows service the debt. Over ~6 years,
        net debt falls from $29.9B to ~$18B
Step 3: KKR begins IPO in 1991, sells remaining stake by mid-1990s.
        EV at exit ~$26B → Equity = $26B − $18B = ~$8B
Result: $1.5B in → ~$8B out over ~6 years = estimated IRR ~14%
        (below PE's typical 20–25% target)
```

> **"Lesson: Even a famous LBO can disappoint. KKR paid too high a multiple;
> tobacco litigation also weighed on exit valuations. The structure worked — the
> entry price did not."**
>
> **"Entry multiple is everything — overpay and no operational improvement will
> save you."**

*Sources cited on the slide: Barbarians at the Gate (Burrough & Helyar, 1989);
**Kaplan (1993) post-buyout study**; KKR public filings.*

**Note the nuance** — the equity did grow more than 5x ($1.5B → $8B). It was the
*six-year hold* that dragged the IRR to ~14%. A useful illustration of why time
matters as much as multiple.

### The bankruptcy study, in more detail — `SOURCE FACT`
> "**Acquisition of Healthy Firms:** The study challenged the industry narrative
> that PE firms primarily target 'distressed' companies to turn them around.
> **Instead, it found that PE firms often acquire healthy companies and
> significantly increase their probability of default by loading them with
> debt.**"
>
> Published in **Finance Research Letters (2021)**. ~20% LBO bankruptcy rate over
> 10 years vs 2% for the control group.

*(`SRC-P-010` attributes the research to California Polytechnic State University;
this deck gives the journal. Both preserved.)*

### Enterprise Value, restated — `SOURCE FACT`
```
Enterprise Value = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash
```
"EV = **TOTAL cost to buy a company — regardless of how it is financed**… **It is
what it actually costs to own 100% of the business.** It is capital-structure
neutral." "EV/EBITDA tells a buyer what they are paying for **operating
performance — not for financing choices.**"

*(Slightly simpler than `SRC-P-008`'s version, which also includes minority
interest. Both are the course's own; neither is "corrected.")*

## WACC and the Cost of Capital

`SOURCE FACT` — **"The WACC is like a company's 'hurdle rate' for investments."**

> **Low WACC Environment → Higher deal volume, higher valuations**
> **High WACC Environment → Lower deal volume, lower valuations**

- **Debt is typically 2/3 of an LBO deal.** "Note: **the interest is tax
  deductible, which makes the debt essentially cheaper.**"
- **Equity is typically 1/3.** "This is essentially the Risk/Return Expected Rate
  from the Capital Appreciation Model (CAPM)." `[sic — CAPM is normally the
  Capital Asset Pricing Model; the deck uses both names]`
- **"Today, the Cost of Debt for a PE Buyout Deal is ~9.6%."** "This can vary
  widely with rates….and strongly affect Private Equity."

### Cost of Equity — CAPM — `SOURCE FACT`
```
Expected Return / Cost of Equity
    = Risk-Free Rate (10 YR GOVT 0 RISK BOND) + (Beta × Market Risk Premium)
    = 14% = 4% + (1.25 × 8.0%)
```
Inputs shown: **10-year government bond 4.019%** · **Beta 1.25x** · equity risk
premium **~6%–10%** — "i.e., ~4% public-market ERP + ~2%–5% illiquidity/PE
premium."

### Beta — `SOURCE FACT`
"Beta measures how much a stock's price moves compared to the overall market
(usually the S&P 500). It's basically a **'volatility score'**."

| Beta | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | Moves about the same as the market | Market +10% → stock ~+10% |
| > 1.0 | More volatile | Beta 1.5 → moves 15% when market moves 10% |
| < 1.0 | Less volatile | Beta 0.5 → moves 5% when market moves 10% |
| Negative | Moves opposite the market | "Rare; could be gold or inverse ETFs" |

### WACC — the worked example — `SOURCE FACT`

**With equity and debt** — a $300M company, $100M equity / $200M debt, 30% tax:
```
(100/300 × 14%) + (200/300 × 9.6% × (1 − 0.30))
= (0.33 × 14%) + (0.66 × 6.72%)
= 9.15%
```

**With equity only** — $300M/$300M equity: **= 14%**

> "WACC — Simple Example — No debt. **CALX.** If no Debt, WACC of capital is just
> the Cost of Equity component, from the Capital Asset Pricing Model."

**The lesson in the contrast:** adding debt drops the cost of capital from 14% to
9.15% — because debt is cheaper than equity *and* its interest is tax-deductible.
That is the arithmetic reason LBOs exist.

## Guitar Center — an LBO followed to bankruptcy

`SOURCE FACT` — "When a deal goes down in value…. Guitar Center: From Leveraged
Buyout to Bankruptcy"

### Timeline
| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1997** | **IPO priced at $15.00 on NASDAQ.** Operated **39 stores**. "Long-term debt was a tool used for store expansion." |
| **2007** | **Bain Capital acquires for $1.9B** (26% premium to prior close). Operated **214 stores**. "Bain refinanced existing debt and raised **~$1.6B of incremental financing, increasing annual interest expense by ~$152M**." |
| **2014** | **Debt restructuring / ownership shift.** "**Ares Management exchanged debt for preferred equity**, taking controlling interest alongside Bain Capital. **~$500M of debt eliminated, reducing annual cash interest expense by ~$70M.**" |
| **2020** | **Chapter 11 bankruptcy** "amid pandemic disruptions and a heavy leveraged balance sheet. Restructuring eliminated **~$800M of ~$1.3B in debt** and secured new financing from **Ares, Brigade, and Carlyle**." |

### Why Bain did the deal — `SOURCE FACT`
> "Guitar Center's store expansion required significant capital, **creating
> pressure on quarterly earnings while public.** Taking the company private
> allowed Bain to theoretically slow expansion or fund growth with leverage,
> **avoiding short-term scrutiny from public markets.**"

### The warning signs already in the financials — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Goodwill impairment** — from acquiring **Music Group (2001)** and **Music &
  Arts Center (2005)**, which brought band and orchestra instruments. "The
  impairment is due to the value of those acquired businesses being no longer
  supported by their projected future cash flow."
- **Inventory scaled faster than revenue** — **"Inventory grew 123% CAGR vs.
  15.8% sales CAGR (2004–2006)."** "**~$2.0M inventory required per new store ×
  33 openings in 2006 = ~$66M incremental inventory.**"

### The financial impact of the LBO — `SOURCE FACT`
1. **"Interest Burden Reduced Financial Flexibility"** — ~$1.6B incremental debt;
   ~$152M annual interest expense "materially reduced net income"
2. **"LBO Interest Burden Weighed on Bottom-Line Performance"**
3. **"LBO Significantly Increased Financial Leverage"** — "dramatically increased
   leverage relative to the company's pre-acquisition capital structure"

And the verdict slide: **"Limited Operational Improvement Under Private Equity
Ownership."**

**Why this case earns its place** — `CLAUDE INFERENCE`: Guitar Center is the
middle case between RJR (over-leveraged disaster) and Marketo (operational
success). Bain had a coherent thesis — go private to escape quarterly pressure
on store expansion — but added leverage without the operational improvement, and
the working-capital intensity flagged in the pre-deal financials (inventory
growing 8x faster than sales) was exactly what sank it. It illustrates the
`SRC-P-008` lesson that **working capital is a cash need that never appears on
the income statement.**

## Key Takeaways

1. **The LBO capital stack is ~50% senior secured / 20% senior unsecured /
   25% junk / 5% PE equity** — and the position in that stack determines both
   rate and who gets paid in bankruptcy.
2. **WACC drops from 14% to 9.15%** when a company is financed 2/3 with debt at
   9.6% and a 30% tax rate. That arithmetic is why leverage exists.
3. **Cost of Equity = Risk-Free Rate + (Beta × Market Risk Premium)**; the PE
   version adds a 2–5% illiquidity premium.
4. **"2x4" (~20% IRR) is the PE hurdle; "4x4" (~40% IRR) is the VC hurdle.**
5. **Entry multiple is everything.** KKR's equity grew 5x on RJR and still only
   returned ~14% IRR, because they paid ~10–11x on an asset that historically
   traded at 5x and held for six years.
6. **PE buys healthy companies and makes them fragile** — the 2021 Finance
   Research Letters study explicitly rejects the distressed-turnaround narrative.
7. **Guitar Center shows leverage without operational improvement** — plus
   working-capital intensity that was visible in the financials beforehand.
8. **The Rule of 40 works on private companies too** — the OpenAI read scores
   ~110% against a 40–60% "strong SaaS" benchmark.
