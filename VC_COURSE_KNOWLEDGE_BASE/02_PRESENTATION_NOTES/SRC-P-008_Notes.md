## Being Public: SEC Mandatory Filings

`SOURCE FACT` — intent: **"ALL investors have equal access to information."**

| Cadence | Filings | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual** | Form 10-K (full-year financial report) · Proxy (DEF 14A, shareholder vote materials) · Annual Report (sent to shareholders) | **60–90 days after fiscal year end** |
| **Quarterly** | Form 10-Q (unaudited quarterly financials) · Earnings Release (filed as 8-K exhibit) · MD&A Update | **40–45 days after quarter end** |
| **Event-driven** | Form 8-K (material events) · Form 4 (insider trading reports) · SC 13D/G (5%+ ownership changes) | **2–4 business days of event** |

### Anatomy of a 10-K — `SOURCE FACT`
"The most comprehensive document a public company must produce each year."
**60–90 day deadline · 200+ average pages · 4 core financial statements.**

- **Part I · Business Overview** — company description, products/services,
  industry, competition, employees, properties
- **Part I · Risk Factors** — material risks to business, financial condition, stock price
- **Part II · Financial Statements** — balance sheet, income statement, cash
  flow, statement of equity — **all audited**
- **Part II · MD&A** — management's analysis of results, liquidity, capital
  resources, trends & outlook
- **Part II · Market for Equity** — stock price history, dividends, holders of
  record, share repurchases
- **Part IV · Exhibits & Certifications** — **CEO/CFO Sarbanes-Oxley
  certifications**, material contracts, subsidiary list

> "Failure to file on time triggers SEC enforcement, stock exchange delisting
> warnings, and potential shareholder litigation."

## Reading an Income Statement

### Gross Margin — `SOURCE FACT`
```
Gross Margin (Gross Profit) = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold
```
Worked example: "if I sell watches: sold one for $100 (Revenue), bought it for
$60 (COGS). Gross Margin = $100 − $60 = **$40, or '40% margin'**."

**The average public company P&L** — `SOURCE FACT`:
```
   38%  Gross Margin
 − 30%  Operating Expenses
 ------
 =  8%  Net Income
```
"They sell $100 of Goods (Revenue), it costs them $62 of COGS, so Gross Margin
is $38, or 38% of Revenue."

A small-company model is described as an **"80/75/5" business model**
`[INTERPRETATION UNCERTAIN]` — the accompanying figures are
`[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]`; by analogy this reads as 80% gross margin, 75%
operating expenses, 5% net income (a high-margin software-like profile).

### Factors affecting gross margin — `SOURCE FACT`
"Strong Business Indicator": pricing power · market conditions · supplier
relations · cost of goods sold · economies of scale · competition · **product
mix (% high margin, % low margin)**.

### Why gross margin matters — `PROFESSOR / COURSE VIEW`
> **"Margin Covers Up a Lot of Sins" (my old boss…)**
>
> "Meaning: Having High Gross Margins allows for less stress on Operating
> Expenses ….including FUN items….. ….you don't need to run the business so
> tightly."

- **High gross margin (like Software)** — "means you can have higher Expenses and
  still make a profit"
- **Low gross margin (like Groceries)** — "means you have to very tightly control
  your expenses, and keep them lower, to make a profit"

`[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]` — "Gross Margin by Industry" chart, with NVDA called out.

### Operating expenses — `SOURCE FACT`
**3 buckets:** Sales and Marketing (S&M) · General and Administrative (G&A) ·
Research and Development (R&D)
**Or 2 buckets:** SG&A · R&D

"SOMETIMES THESE ARE TWO SEPARATE CATEGORIES!" R&D is "not just new products, but
supporting improvements in existing ones too."

```
Revenue − Cost of Revenue = Gross Profit
Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = Operating Income
```

### Depreciation & Amortization — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Depreciation** — "allocate the cost of purchasing a **tangible** asset (i.e.
  capital expenditure, or 'Capex') across its useful life assumption"
- **Amortization** — "allocate the cost of **intangible** assets, such as
  patents, trademarks, and copyrights, over the useful life assumption"

Both flagged on the income statement as **"NOT a cash expense"** and **"NOT an
expense from operations."**

**Where D&A sits** — `SOURCE FACT`:
- **In COGS** when it relates to assets directly used in production (factory
  machinery, manufacturing facilities). *Example given: NVIDIA chip factories and IP.*
- **In OpEx/SG&A** when it relates to assets supporting general business
  operations (office equipment, corporate software, sales vehicles)
- **"Could be both."**

### EBITDA — `SOURCE FACT`
> "EBITDA is much closer to how much Income the business is **GENERATING**. Takes
> out non-cash and non-operating items. **THIS IS THE MOST COMMONLY USED PRIVATE
> EQUITY MEASURE.**"
>
> "**'Add back'** these numbers → get what the business is 'throwing off' in
> Earnings from Operations. This is the primary number used in PE deals — it's
> closest to 'what cash the company is throwing off from its operations'."

## Cash needs that are NOT on the income statement

`SOURCE FACT` — "Even if you're forecasting losses and have cash for that, you
also need extra cash for working capital (because receivables, inventory, etc.
balloon when you grow)."

**Working capital:** "When you grow sales rapidly….. You must buy more inventory
/ materials now. You can't collect from customers immediately. So you need
additional working capital to fund that growth — **even if your gross margins
are healthy.**"

> **"The Valley of Death isn't the only cash need…"**

**Capital expenditure:** "OpEx is in Income Statement. **CapEx is NOT on Income
Statement.** It is an Investing Activity on the Cash Flow statement, and is
depreciated."

> "IT TAKES CAPITAL EXPENDITURES TO KEEP GROWING AND MAINTAINING, IN ADDITION TO
> WORKING CAPITAL…. IN ADDITION TO OPERATING LOSSES"

`[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]` — percentages 7%, 56%, 47%, 113%, 41% appear without
labels, presumably a CapEx-intensity-by-industry chart.

**This is an important three-part cash lesson** — `CLAUDE INFERENCE`: a startup
needs cash for (1) operating losses, (2) working capital to fund growth, and
(3) capex. A founder who models only the P&L loss will under-raise.

## Enterprise Value — the PE metric

### The formula — `SOURCE FACT`
```
EV = Market Cap + Net Debt + Minority Interest − Cash
```
- **Market Cap** — share price × diluted shares outstanding. "Represents equity
  holders' claim only."
- **+ Total Debt** — all interest-bearing obligations (bonds, term loans,
  revolvers). "A buyer assumes this."
- **+ Minority Interest** — third-party stake in consolidated subsidiaries
- **− Cash & Equivalents** — "Acquirer effectively gets this cash back — so it
  offsets the purchase price"

### Why PE ignores market cap — `SOURCE FACT`, four reasons
1. **PE Buys the Whole Company** — "acquire 100% of a business — equity AND debt.
   Market Cap only values equity. EV captures the full acquisition cost."
2. **Capital Structure Neutrality** — "Two identical businesses with different
   debt loads have the same EV but wildly different market caps. EV allows
   apples-to-apples comparison."
3. **LBO Pricing is EV-Based** — "the PE firm negotiates an EV (the 'total
   enterprise price'). Then they decide how much debt vs. equity to use."
4. **Exit Multiples Use EV Too** — "Return calculations require consistency —
   **EV in, EV out.**"

> "Note: EV is capital-structure-neutral. **It is the PE firm's true cost of
> ownership.**"

### EBITDA-based valuation multiples — `SOURCE FACT`

"EBITDA proxies for operating cash flow and strips out financing & accounting
differences — making it **the universal PE denominator**. Approximates
unlevered, pre-tax operating cash flow."

| Multiple | Purpose | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EV / EBITDA** | Entry & exit multiple | **8x – 14x** | "The most widely used PE metric." Benchmarks deals against comparables; sets target exit multiples at acquisition. |
| **EV / Revenue** | Top-line valuation | **1x – 5x+** | Used when EBITDA is negative or depressed (turnaround, early growth). Common in tech/SaaS buyouts. "Less reliable standalone — always paired with margin analysis." |
| **EV / EBIT** | Operating income multiple | **10x – 18x** | Includes D&A, "penalizing capex-heavy businesses." Preferred in capital-light sectors. |
| **P / E** | Equity-level multiple | **12x – 25x** | "Rarely used by PE firms for deal pricing (capital-structure-dependent)," but appears in public comps and LP return measurement at IPO exit. |
| **EV / (EBITDA − CapEx)** | Free cash flow proxy | **10x – 16x** | "Critical for manufacturing, infrastructure, and industrial buyouts where D&A materially understates cash needs." |
| **EV / Adj. EBITDA** | Normalized earnings | Varies by deal | **"PE firms always negotiate 'Adjusted' EBITDA — adding back one-time costs, management fees, non-recurring items. Creates the basis for purchase price. Adjustments are often heavily contested."** |

**The Adjusted EBITDA row is the practitioner's note** — the purchase price is
built on a negotiated, contested number, not an objective one.

## LBO-Specific Metrics

`SOURCE FACT` — "In a Leveraged Buyout, EBITDA is the anchor for **both how much
debt can be raised and whether the business can service it.**"

### Leverage metrics

| Metric | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Total Debt / EBITDA** ("turns of EBITDA") | LBO entry **4x – 7x**; lender limit ~6–7x | **"The single most important LBO metric."** How many years of EBITDA to repay all debt. "Higher leverage = higher return potential but higher default risk. **PE firms target 5–6x at close.**" |
| **Senior Debt / EBITDA** | **3x – 4.5x** senior only | "Banks typically cap senior leverage at 3–4.5x. Anything above must be financed with **mezzanine, second-lien, or PIK notes** at higher rates." |
| **Net Debt / EBITDA** | Covenant tested quarterly | "Used in maintenance covenants — if it rises above a threshold (e.g. **6.5x**) during the hold period, it triggers a **covenant breach** and lender action." |

### Coverage & return metrics

| Metric | Threshold | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **EBITDA / Interest Expense** (Interest Coverage) | Minimum covenant **2.0x – 3.0x** | "**Below 2x is distress territory.** PE firms model this through the hold period under stress scenarios." |
| **(EBITDA − CapEx) / Interest** (FCCR) | Minimum **1.1x – 1.25x** | "More conservative… Preferred by lenders in capex-heavy industries (logistics, healthcare facilities, manufacturing)." |
| **MOIC** | PE target **2.5x – 3.5x+ over 5 years** | "Exit proceeds ÷ equity invested. **EBITDA growth and multiple expansion are the two biggest drivers.**" |
| **IRR** | PE hurdle **20%+ gross IRR** | "A 3x MOIC in 3 years (~44% IRR) is far better than 3x in 7 years (~17% IRR). LPs judge fund performance on IRR. **Leverage amplifies IRR by reducing equity required at entry.**" |

## What Private Equity Is

`SOURCE FACT` — "investment capital that is not typically listed on a public
exchange. PE firms and funds acquire **full ownership** in companies. If 'buying
out' a public company….the PE investors may take the company private…. change
them…. go public again potential. **The 'Flip'…..**"

### The three basic steps — `SOURCE FACT`
```
BUY ENTIRE COMPANY  (full control attained)
        ↓
CHANGE IT  and/or  LEVER IT
        ↓
SELL at HIGHER EQUITY VALUE
```

### PE deal mix today — `SOURCE FACT`
| Type | Share |
|---|---|
| Leveraged Buyouts (LBOs) — "include financial engineering to amplify returns" | **~25–30%** |
| Buyouts — **"focus on undervalued companies, not financial engineering"** | **~25–30%** |
| Growth Capital / Venture — "with a recent decline in venture capital activity" | **~20%** |
| Market Expansion — "notably in the industrials sector" | **~15%** |
| Real Estate centric (REIT) | **~10%** |

### Structure — same as VC, with one difference — `SOURCE FACT`
- **General Partners (GPs)** — "the professional investment managers who make
  investment decisions." *Same as VC.*
- **Limited Partners (LPs)** — "institutional investors and wealthy individuals
  who provide capital." *Same as VC.*
- **Portfolio companies** — "the complete businesses that private equity firms
  acquire and attempt to improve." **"DIFFERENT THAN VC…. buy COMPANIES, not
  stakes in startups."**

**Fund economics: "2 and 20"** — "2% management fee on committed" capital, and
"20% of profits above a **hurdle rate**/fund payback — 'carried interest'."
"Private equity funds are typically structured like VC funds."

`[VISUAL NOT CAPTURED]` — "RETURNS for TOP QUARTILE FIRMS: VC and PE and REIT are
all 'Private Equity' Subcategories. Note the return variations……" and "HOW PE
FIRMS INCREASE EBITDA, EV, for EXIT."

## WORKED DEAL 2 — The LBO that makes money with zero growth

`SOURCE FACT` — the single clearest illustration of leverage in the course.

**Entry:** XYZ Company purchased by PE at a **$100M** valuation.
```
Debt Raised:               $80M  (80%)  — 7 year loan
Private Equity:            $15M  (15%)  — "small…"
Management Contribution:    $5M   (5%)  — "even smaller……"

Post-Buyout Capital Structure:  Equity $20M · Debt $80M
Debt Repayment Strategy: Paid using company profits over time
```

**Exit at Year 7 — sold at the SAME $100M valuation. No valuation increase at all.**
```
$80M debt  →  now ZERO (paid off by the company's cash flow)
$20M equity → now owns 100% of the company = $100M

PE:         $15M of $20M equity = 75% × $100M = $75M
            $75M / $15M = 3.75x their money  — "a good deal!"
Management:  $5M of $20M equity = 25% × $100M = $25M
            $25M / $5M = 4x their money — "an even better deal!"
```

> **"THIS TYPE OF DEAL is VERY DEPENDENT On the COST OF DEBT though…."**

**Why this example is so good** — `CLAUDE INFERENCE`: it isolates leverage as the
sole return driver. The company did not grow, the multiple did not expand, and
investors still made 3.75–4x — purely because the *company's own cash flow*
retired debt that sat ahead of their equity. It also shows why management often
does better than the sponsor per dollar invested.

### How the debt works — `SOURCE FACT`
> "Basically you pay with Equity Investment (typically about **1/3** of the
> price) AND you pay with Debt (typically about **2/3**). When the debt is paid
> off…..that 1/3 of the purchase price in equity……now owns 100% of the company.
>
> **Who pays the debt? The company that is acquired does** — hence EBITDA TO PAY
> DEBT is important.
>
> Can they afford it? ........sometimes yes….sometimes it kills the company….
> **LEVERAGE AMPLIFIES…WHETHER GOOD OR BAD**"

**Debt vs leverage** — `SOURCE FACT`: "they are related. **Debt** refers to money
owed. **Leverage** is the use of borrowed funds (debt) to potentially increase
investment returns, also amplifying potential losses."

**Amount of debt in an LBO:** "Typically, private equity firms aim for a
significant amount of debt, often between **50% to 90%** of the purchase price.
**Average LBO Deal is 1/3 Equity, 2/3 Debt Leverage.**"

**PE deals have positive cash flows, unlike VC** — which is precisely what makes
debt possible.

## Barbarians at the Gate / RJR Nabisco

### Why RJR was a target — `SOURCE FACT`
- "The company had **strong cash flow from its tobacco business**, making it a
  prime candidate for an LBO"
- **"The Market didn't reward them for better and better financial results…..
  ITS A TOBACCO COMPANY……became a liability"**
- "Management thought they could make more money for themselves by taking it over"
- On the conglomerate logic: "RJR was the largest TOBACCO company — facing
  headwinds. Nabisco was a wholesome cookie company….. Together they could be a
  conglomerate not viewed as a tobacco company… **WRONG.**"

### Management buyouts (MBOs) — `SOURCE FACT`
"MANAGEMENT OFTEN LEADS BUYOUTS - A MANAGEMENT BUYOUT OR MBO..IF UNDERVALUED"
1. Management expresses interest in acquiring the business
2. Valuation is determined through financial analysis
3. Funding is secured (bank loans, private equity, seller financing)
4. Negotiations and due diligence are conducted
5. Transaction closes, and management takes control

> **"WHY? BECAUSE THEY FEEL UNDERVALUED IN THE MARKET……"**

### The deal — `SOURCE FACT`
- **1988 LBO of RJR Nabisco for $25 billion** — the largest LBO at the time
- **"The final price was $31.1 billion"**
- Structured as a **Leveraged Management Led Buyout (LMBO)**
- **CEO Ross Johnson** initially led the buyout attempt, partnering with
  **Shearson Lehman**
- **KKR (Kohlberg Kravis Roberts)** and **First Boston** entered the bidding war
- "Debt Financing: The LBO relied heavily on **junk bonds** and debt"

### What to watch for — deal dynamics — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Conflict of Interest** — "Johnson's role as CEO and bidder raised ethical concerns"
- **Hostile vs. Friendly Takeover** — "KKR's bid turned the process into a hostile takeover"
- **Bidding War** — "The auction-style process drove up the price unsustainably"
- **Due Diligence Failures** — "Excitement over the deal led to underestimating operational risks"
- **Strategic Mistakes** — "Overconfidence in RJR Nabisco's cash flow led to over-leveraging"
- **Golden Parachutes** — "Executives ensured their payouts regardless of the deal's success"

### Why the deal was a disaster — `SOURCE FACT`
- **Crippling Debt** — "reduced its ability to invest in growth"
- **Failed Synergies** — "Promised efficiencies and cost savings never materialized"
- **Management Shakeups** — "KKR installed new leadership, but strategic direction was unclear"
- **Fire Sale of Assets** — "To manage debt, parts sold off piecemeal. **DIVESTITURES.**"
- **Investor Losses** — "the deal failed to generate expected returns"
- **"Cautionary Tale: A lesson in financial overreach and corporate greed"**

## Key Takeaways

1. **EBITDA is the PE denominator** — closest proxy to cash the business throws
   off. Adjusted EBITDA is negotiated and contested, and it sets the price.
2. **EV, not market cap** — PE buys the whole company including debt.
   EV in, EV out.
3. **Total Debt/EBITDA is the single most important LBO metric** — 4–7x entry,
   5–6x target, ~6–7x lender ceiling.
4. **An LBO can return 3.75x with zero growth and zero multiple expansion** —
   the company's own cash flow retires the debt. Leverage amplifies both ways.
5. **The company pays the debt**, which is why PE needs profitable targets and
   VC cannot use this structure.
6. **Three cash needs, only one on the P&L** — operating losses, working
   capital, capex.
7. **Median PE return over 10 years is 13.5%** — the bar a deal must beat.
8. **Gross margin "covers up a lot of sins."**
9. **RJR Nabisco is the anti-example** — bidding war, conflicted management,
   over-leverage against overconfident cash flow assumptions.
