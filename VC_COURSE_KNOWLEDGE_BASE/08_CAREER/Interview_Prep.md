# VC Interview Preparation

**Version:** v1.0 — 2026-08-12. Answers grounded in **actual course material**,
with source IDs so every claim can be traced before it is said out loud.

---

## The rule
**Never claim professional investing experience.** The honest framing —
*"in my venture capital coursework we worked through…"* — is credible and, said
confidently with real numbers behind it, lands better than vagueness dressed up
as experience.

---

## "What's the difference between VC and PE?"
**The strongest answer available**, straight from `SRC-P-001`:

> "They're often lumped together, but the processes are completely different.
> Venture funds unprofitable, early-stage companies through what my professor
> called the valley of death — the domain of companies that don't yet make money.
> PE buys profitable companies, usually with debt the acquired company itself
> repays. That's why PE can use leverage and venture generally can't.
>
> My professor had a metaphor I've never forgotten: **investment bankers are like
> salespeople — paid on transaction volume, predictably. VCs are like bettors who
> sell the house they funded — slow, unpredictable, massive upside. PE investors
> are house flippers who can sell profitably even without appreciation.**
>
> It shows up in the return math too: a good VC fund targets 40% IRR, a good PE
> fund 20%. VC needs the same multiple in half the time."

## "How do you value an early-stage company?"
> "You don't use DCF — there's often no cash flow to discount. The method I
> learned is the **venture capital method**: you work backwards from the return
> you need.
>
> Say a company could reach $100M of revenue and exit in four years, and the
> sector trades at 3x revenue. That's a $300M exit. If I need 40% IRR over four
> years, I need 4x. So $300M divided by 4 is $75M — that's the most I can pay
> post-money, or less.
>
> Then you sanity-check against market averages by round — NVCA and PitchBook
> track those. Comparables, not intrinsic value, drive venture pricing."

## "Explain dilution."
> "The arithmetic is simple: **pre-money plus the investment equals post-money,
> and the investment divided by post-money is the ownership the investor buys** —
> which is the founders' dilution.
>
> The interesting part is timing. We worked an exercise where a company raised
> $40M two ways. **Staged**: $18M at a $12M pre, then $22M at a $100M pre.
> **All at once**: the full $40M at the $12M valuation. Same capital.
>
> Staged, each founder walked away with **$16.9M**. All at once, **$12.4M** —
> about 40% less. Investors went from about 9x to 12x.
>
> The lesson is that **capital isn't the scarce resource — the valuation you sell
> it at is.** Raise what you need to reach the next valuation step."

## "Explain a cap table / what terms matter?"
> "The terms split into economics and control.
>
> On economics, **liquidation preference** matters most. 1x non-participating is
> standard; participating, capped participating, and 3x exist and are
> investor-favorable. Preferences **cumulate by round**, which produces a trap
> people miss: we looked at a company that raised $40M and got a $38M offer —
> the preference stack eats everything and the common is worth roughly nothing.
>
> Anti-dilution matters in down rounds — weighted average is normal, full ratchet
> is punitive.
>
> On control: protective provisions, board seats, registration rights,
> **pro rata** — my professor's line was *'you can't kick them out of the
> syndicate.'*
>
> One thing worth knowing: **at IPO, preferred converts automatically and the
> liquidation preference is forfeited.** So it's downside protection that
> disappears in the best outcome — it shapes behavior in mediocre exits."

## "How does a VC make money?"
> "Two streams: **2% of the fund annually in management fees regardless of
> performance, and 20% carried interest** above returning capital and fees. The
> fund runs ten years, and the GP typically has to commit 1% of the fund
> themselves.
>
> The carry is where the money is, but it's concentrated. We studied Sevin Rosen's
> first fund — **$25M, and it held both Compaq and Lotus.** Gross proceeds about
> $191M. One fund, two historic companies.
>
> The constraint that explains VC behavior is timing: it takes about **$75M and
> three rounds and eight years** to get a company public, inside a **ten-year**
> fund. There's almost no slack, which is why exit pressure is real."

## "What makes an attractive venture investment?"
Structure on the market/product/deal/exit spine, then close with:
> "Ultimately I'd underwrite to 4x in four years or 5x in five — roughly 40% IRR
> — knowing that **about three-quarters of venture deals don't return capital at
> all.** You're not trying to be right often; you're trying to be very right
> occasionally."

## "Tell me about a company you analyzed."
**Compaq (`SRC-P-007`)** — the strongest option:
> "We did a full teardown of Compaq. Three institutional rounds after seed —
> $2.5M, $5M, then $25M, led by Sevin Rosen with IVP joining later. **No bank
> debt at all** — the documentary we watched got that wrong and my professor
> corrected it.
>
> At IPO it was about a **$500M market cap on $111M of revenue** — roughly 5x
> revenue — and each of the three founders held only **5–7%**. That's the
> dilution lesson in real numbers.
>
> What I found most interesting is what came after. Fastest company to the
> Fortune 500, passed IBM by 1995 — then a **$9.6 billion acquisition of DEC in
> 1998 that my professor described as a bad fit**, and HP bought them for **$25
> billion** in 2002. A home run at IPO doesn't mean a good company forever."

## "Walk me through an LBO."
> "The cleanest example we did: a company bought at **$100M — $80M of debt, $15M
> from the sponsor, $5M from management.** Seven years later it sells at **the
> same $100M**. No growth, no multiple expansion.
>
> But the debt was paid down by the company's own cash flow. So the $20M of
> equity now owns 100% of a $100M company. **The sponsor turned $15M into $75M —
> 3.75x. Management turned $5M into $25M — 4x.** Purely from debt paydown.
>
> That's the mechanic. The caution is the other side: **leverage amplifies
> whether good or bad.** About 20% of large LBOs go bankrupt within ten years,
> against 2% for comparable companies."

## "What's the difference between good and bad PE?"
> "We studied both deliberately. **RJR Nabisco** — KKR controlled a $31.4 billion
> company with $1.5 billion of equity, only about 0.3% of which was KKR's own
> money. Roughly 95% debt. They paid around 10–11x EBITDA for an asset that
> historically traded at 5x. The equity grew from $1.5B to about $8B — but over
> six years that's only **~14% IRR**, below the 20% they'd target. **Entry
> multiple is everything.**
>
> Against that, **Vista and Marketo**: bought for $1.79B with only **22% debt**,
> installed a new CEO, cut bloated sales spend, took net margin from **−31% to
> positive**, and sold to Adobe for **$4.75B in 25 months** — about 3.1x, ~72%
> IRR. Adobe paid 14.8x revenue because it needed to beat Salesforce.
>
> The difference is leverage versus operations. And there's a current reason
> that matters: my professor cited Bain's framing — **'12 is the new 5.'**
> Without cheap debt, PE now needs 12% EBITDA growth where 5% used to do."

## "How would you source deals?"
> "The thing that stuck with me is that **sourcing channel affects returns**.
> Banker-led processes are auctions — they generate more bids and bid the price
> up. Proprietary deals return better. LPs actually select on this; one quoted in
> our material said *'we only invest in managers who don't rely solely on
> bankers.'*
>
> The screening method I learned: pick a space, find a best-in-class company as
> your reference for what good trades at, then work down the list looking for
> companies trading well below that multiple and ask whether something is broken
> or just not scaling."

## "Why venture capital?"
Use his closing framework (`SRC-P-014`) — it is honest and it is his:
> "My professor ended the course by asking which of these we actually were.
> Bankers do 7+ deals a year and get paid on volume. PE investors are financial
> engineers with a 3–5 year attention span. VCs are **patient bettors in tech,
> high risk, very high reward, willing to wait ten years.**
>
> The venture answer is the one I recognized. I like the analytical work, but
> what draws me is the time horizon and building alongside companies rather than
> transacting on them."

## The question you should raise yourself — the Iowa angle
> "One thing from the course I've kept thinking about: **PE firms have been
> buying insurers and redirecting their portfolios into private credit the PE
> firm itself originates, manages, values, and charges fees at every step.** The
> example we used was **Global Atlantic in Des Moines, which KKR bought in 2021**
> — and it's all backed by ordinary policyholders' retirement savings.
>
> That's not abstract for me. I work in retirement income in Iowa, so I see the
> client side of exactly those products. Iowa is a real insurance and retirement
> capital centre — Principal, Transamerica, and a long list of others. I think
> that's an underrated place to develop a differentiated view."

**Use this.** It is specific, current, personally grounded, and no other
candidate has it.

## Technical facts worth having instant
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| VC hurdle | 40% IRR — 4x/4yr, 5x/5yr |
| PE hurdle | 20% IRR — 2x/4yr |
| Fund-level survival | 20%+ IRR or no next fund |
| PE entry multiple | ~11x EV/EBITDA (corporate M&A ~9x) |
| LBO leverage | 4–7x Debt/EBITDA, target 5–6x |
| Standard liq pref | 1x non-participating |
| Common anti-dilution | Weighted average |
| Escrow in M&A | 8–15%, 1–2 years |
| Rule of 40 | Growth% + margin% ≥ 40%, using FCF/EBITDA |
| Deals sold vs IPO | ~90% acquired |
| VC deals returning capital | ~25% |
| M&A destroying value | ~47% |
